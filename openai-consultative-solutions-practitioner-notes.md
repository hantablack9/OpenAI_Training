# OpenAI Consultative Solutions Practitioner — concise notes

## Recognizing Strong AI Opportunities

- General AI interest is a starting signal, not an opportunity. Translate labels such as “we need AI,” “chatbot,” or “copilot” into a concrete workflow problem.
- Use the qualification lens: **problem → workflow → owner → improvement**. Ask what is happening today, where friction appears, who experiences/owns it, and what would improve.
- Strong opportunity evidence covers five categories: business-problem clarity, stakeholder visibility, use-case direction, customer commitment, and a credible reason the improvement matters.
- Readiness is evidence-based:
  - **Lead:** interest or a possible problem, but the opportunity is not yet clear.
  - **Stage 0:** structured validation is justified; the problem, plausible use case, and stakeholders/next steps are emerging.
  - **Stage 1:** enough evidence supports deeper joint qualification; solution, ROI, architecture, and commercial details may still be open.
- A strong first next step is specific: a scheduled workflow session, named stakeholders, shared sample data/process detail, or a defined validation/pilot discussion. “Let’s reconnect sometime” is weak commitment.
- Value framing should be a hypothesis chain: workflow problem → expected improvement → business outcome. Avoid promising ROI before validation.
- “Conditionally ready” is useful when the core problem, owner, and next validation action are clear but success measures, approvals, or stakeholder alignment still need work.
- If evidence is weak, clarify rather than advance: identify the workflow and owner, bound the first use case, state what is unknown, and choose the next evidence-building action.

## One-line takeaway

Progress opportunities on evidence, not enthusiasm: make the workflow, owner, use-case direction, commitment, and reason to act clear enough that the next qualification step is defensible.

## Discovery That Drives Qualification

- Discovery is not a product pitch; turn broad AI requests into evidence about current work, workflow friction, owners, impact, readiness, and the next validation step.
- Start with the problem: map where the workflow slows, which systems, people, and handoffs are involved, and distinguish facts from assumptions.
- Strong questions are curious, workflow-centered, evidence-seeking, and collaborative. Listen for vague answers, ask follow-ups, reflect back, and test tentative hypotheses.
- Identify affected users, the outcome owner, influencers, sponsors, and governance participants; different roles define success differently.
- Readiness shows through action: clear ownership, engaged stakeholders, willingness to share examples, and a concrete workflow review or validation session.
- Surface operating context early—data sensitivity, approvals, compliance, human review, oversight, and policy constraints—without jumping into solution design.
- Summarize findings in six fields: customer problem, workflow pain, stakeholders/owner, impact signal, readiness/operating context, and next validation step. Advance only when evidence supports the next action.

## Designing High-Impact Use Cases

- Translate discovery into a specific workflow opportunity: name the process step, user, work object, AI-supported output, human role, and desired outcome—not just a product label.
- Look for repeatable patterns such as retrieval, extraction, classification/routing, generation, review, recommendations, coordination, and monitoring. These are opportunity lenses, not requirements to automate everything.
- Evaluate candidates on workflow friction and business relevance, readiness of people/process/information, measurement clarity, risk and controls, and scope/complexity.
- Prefer a narrow, observable, reviewable starting point with human oversight and a fast path to evidence. Higher-risk decision or escalation use cases may be valuable follow-ons once confidence and governance improve.
- Compare candidates consistently: expected workflow improvement, stakeholder support, validation practicality/speed, risk, measurability, and expansion potential. Explain both the recommendation and the trade-offs.
- Define success signals before deeper value discussions. They should be relevant to the workflow problem, meaningful to stakeholders, observable in validation, and connected to outcomes—not merely model quality or AI enthusiasm.
- A useful validation statement is: “We will evaluate whether [use case] improves [workflow outcome] for [stakeholder] by observing [evidence].”
- Example signals for policy retrieval: less search time, more consistent responses, fewer avoidable escalations, easier onboarding, and positive agent feedback. Tie each signal to the workflow step, stakeholder, AI output, human role, and desired outcome.

## Building the Business Value

- Build value in sequence: AI capability → workflow problem → specific use case/improvement → operational outcome → customer business priority → evidence → next validation action.
- A use case says what AI might help do; a value story explains why it is worth pursuing. Start with the customer’s work, not labels such as “assistant,” “agent,” or “document AI.”
- Choose one outcome that best matches the customer’s current priority. Use outcome areas as prompts: revenue capacity, cost, productive capacity, speed, quality, risk/control, customer experience, and employee experience.
- A value hypothesis is testable, not a promise or ROI model: “Because [workflow problem] affects [stakeholder/outcome], improving [step/output] with AI could help [measurable improvement], which matters because [customer priority].”
- Separate evidence from assumptions. Evidence includes volume, effort/cost, cycle time, error/rework/escalation rates, stakeholder priorities, examples, sample work, and workflow ownership. Assumptions include adoption, baseline performance, data access, oversight, deployment/change effort, and how saved time converts to value.
- Use precise language: describe what could improve and what to validate; avoid unsupported claims such as “this will reduce cost,” “ROI will be strong,” or “AI will transform the business.”
- Measure only what supports the value story. Match indicators to the outcome (for example, cycle time for speed, manual effort/cases for capacity, rework/error for quality, escalations/audit findings for risk, response time/resolution for customer experience).
- Directional benefit estimates are early sizing checks, not ROI. Use customer-validated inputs (e.g., volume × current effort × expected reduction), name weak assumptions, and use the result to choose the next sales action. ROI additionally needs cost, implementation, adoption, time horizon, operating cost, risk/confidence, and value-conversion assumptions.
- Do not estimate when volume, baseline, expected improvement, outcome, or customer priority is unknown; gather the missing evidence instead.
- Tailor emphasis without changing facts: CFO—cost/productivity/investment confidence; COO—throughput/cycle time/service; CIO/CTO—scale, integration, governance/data/control; workflow owner—usability, quality, adoption, handoffs; executive sponsor—strategic priority, urgency, impact, and risk of inaction.
- Keep portfolio references light and separate value hypothesis, solution direction, and validation need. End with a specific, evidence-building next step.

## Mapping Solutions & Advancing the Opportunity

- Route from workflow evidence, not labels such as “agent,” “copilot,” “automation,” or “API.” Clarify who does the work, what is slow/risky/inconsistent, where AI interacts, what output should improve, and what value matters.
- Separate four decisions: **user/product surface** (where work happens), **capability/architecture** (what the system must do), **deployment/operating model** (where/how it runs and is controlled), and **progression motion** (what happens next).
- Surface signals: ChatGPT Chat fits collaborative exploration and drafting; ChatGPT Work fits delegated multi-step knowledge work with user steering/approval; an API-based application fits embedded product, system, or operational workflows; Codex fits software delivery and engineering work.
- Capability signals include apps/tools, retrieval, structured outputs, code execution, automation, multi-step action, scheduling, approvals, and exception handling. “Agentic” describes required behavior, not a product surface.
- Validate deployment requirements before recommending self-managed/open-weight operation. Check hosted controls first; confirm data boundaries, residency, offline needs, permissions, approvals, retention, logging, monitoring, human review, and the customer’s ability to operate the system.
- Combined/hybrid solutions need a clear business reason. Name which surface, capability, and operating model each workflow part requires instead of using “hybrid” to avoid a decision.
- Test fit separately from readiness. Readiness includes workflow/priority clarity, stakeholder ownership, AI maturity, governance/approvals, data/integration access, adoption and success measures, an explicit value hypothesis, and a customer who can support validation.
- Use tradeoffs to route responsibly: cost, latency, integration, data sensitivity, governance, adoption, complexity, and human review/reversibility. Business tradeoffs stay in value/stakeholder alignment; technical, security, data, or governance questions go to validation or handoff.
- Next-step logic: weak problem/owner/value → more discovery; clear use case/value but uncertain requirements → validation session; material technical/integration/security/governance questions → technical or deployment handoff; replacement/consolidation → migration discovery; clear evidence/readiness/routing → structured handoff.
- A concise handoff records workflow/use case, customer and partner owners, evidence, value hypothesis, likely surface and alternatives, capability/architecture and operating requirements, known facts vs assumptions, open questions/risks, and recommended next action/owner. The goal is to move forward without restarting discovery.

## Vertical Solution Application

- Start with the workflow output, not the product or industry label. Identify whether AI should retrieve/synthesize knowledge, extract or structure information, draft/transform content, classify/route work, review outputs, recommend actions, coordinate steps, or support software delivery.
- Read the vertical context around the workflow: work object and friction, data boundaries, stakeholders and decision owners, governance/review needs, operating ownership, and success measures. The same review/synthesis pattern can require different evidence in finance, healthcare, and life sciences.
- Keep three routing axes separate: user/product surface, required capability/architecture, and deployment/operations. ChatGPT Chat supports collaborative thinking and drafting; ChatGPT Work supports delegated knowledge work; Codex supports software engineering; an API-built application/process fits embedded workflows. Agentic behavior is a capability, not a peer product route.
- Financial-services risk review: validate sensitive financial data classes, systems of record, policy consistency, auditability, committee/review ownership, approval steps, permissions, logging, and success metrics. A likely next step is validation before handoff.
- Healthcare administrative intake: confirm approved data boundaries, whether sensitive health information is involved, whether the workflow is administrative/operational/clinical, who approves use, where human review is required, and system ownership. If those facts are unclear, qualify further; if clear, hand off with the evidence.
- Life-sciences knowledge synthesis: validate source access, evidence quality/standards, source traceability, scientific or regulatory review, domain-owner validation, data sensitivity, controlled access, human review, success metrics, and explicit infrastructure requirements. Evaluate hosted controls before considering open-weight or self-managed deployment.
- Value changes by stakeholder: executives may care about growth, efficiency, risk, or strategic change; business owners about throughput, quality, backlog, speed, CX, or resilience; governance about auditability, approved use, access, human review, and traceability; technical teams about integration and operations; end users about usability, rework, time saved, and confidence.
- Be specific about trust/readiness: document regulated or sensitive data, human-review points, source traceability/audit trails, systems and repositories, permissions/approvals, operating owners, and the metric that proves value. Do not make legal, regulatory, security, architecture, API, migration, or self-managed infrastructure decisions; bring qualified validators in.
- Seller recommendation order: (1) state the routing read across surface, capabilities, and operations; (2) name what could change or extend it; (3) choose the next step. Weak evidence calls for qualification; credible routing with unresolved constraints calls for validation; clear workflow/readiness calls for technical or deployment handoff; replacement of an existing solution calls for migration discovery.
- Pitch pattern: “Because [workflow] in [vertical context] and because [data/stakeholder/governance/review factor] matters, the likely surface is [surface], capabilities are [capabilities], operating requirements to validate are [requirements], and the next step is [next step].” Keep the direction provisional and do not claim unresolved requirements are solved.
- Concise handoff fields: workflow/work object, vertical evidence, stakeholders/owners, value hypothesis, likely surface and alternatives, required capabilities, operating/deployment requirements, known facts vs assumptions, open validation questions, next owner/action.

## Early Opportunity Progression Application

- Early opportunities are decision points, not handoffs. Interest and urgency are signals, not proof of readiness.
- Qualify the workflow before routing a product: current process, affected users, owner, measurable impact, desired outcome, narrow first use case, other stakeholders, and a committed next step.
- Five qualification signals: business-problem clarity, stakeholders identified, initial AI use case framed, customer engagement/commitment, and an early value hypothesis. Rate each strong, developing, or missing.
- Riverton example: incomplete claims packets, inconsistent severity/triage, adjusters reconstruct context from claims systems, email, notes, and photos; likely first use case is first-pass claim review/routing support with human review for coverage, liability, payment, and exception decisions.
- Do not automate high-stakes insurance decisions; keep qualified human review and accountability visible.
- A credible Stage 1 recommendation needs enough evidence for structured follow-up, not perfect information. Separate known facts, assumptions, and validation needs.
- Seller-level routing remains three axes: surface (ChatGPT Chat/Work, Codex, or API-built embedded workflow), required capability/architecture (retrieval, structured extraction, connected tools, or multi-step work), and deployment/operations (identity, permissions, approvals, logging, retention, human review, and ownership).
- Validate representative de-identified packets, claim volume/cycle-time/backlog/rework baselines, systems/data access, success measures, review/approval boundaries, and stakeholders from claims systems, data, security, and legal.
- Strong next step: a focused workflow-validation session with a named owner, sample data/process, success measures, and required stakeholders. Do not jump to final architecture, ROI guarantees, or a product pitch.
